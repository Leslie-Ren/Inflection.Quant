From Option Prices to the Shape of Returns: A Model-Free Construction of Volatility, Skewness and Kurtosis

April 9, 2026

Why This Matters#

Most of my early intuition about options came from the Black Scholes model, which is clean and widely used. But once I started working with real option surfaces, it becomes clear that the Black Scholes assumption of a lognormal distribution is too restrictive. For a given maturity, the implied volatility is not constant across strikes, and its shape suggests asymmetry and heavy tails in the risk-neutral distribution.

That leads to a more basic question. Instead of imposing a parametric model and calibrating its parameters, is there a way to extract volatility, skewness, and kurtosis directly from option prices in a model-free way? This is where the Bakshi Kapadia Madan (2003) framework becomes useful. Their key idea is that smooth payoff functions can be represented as a continuum of vanilla options across strikes. In this view, volatility, skewness, and kurtosis are not model assumptions or calibration outputs. They are quantities that can be recovered from market prices through static option replication.

This article is my attempt to explain the math behind their ideas. But before getting into their formulas, I first revisit a simple form of Taylor’s theorem, which turns out to be the key foundation behind their representation.

Taylor’s Theorem with Integral Remainder#

The standard Taylor expansion approximates a smooth function around a point using derivatives evaluated at that point. For our purposes, the most useful form is the first-order expansion with integral remainder, because it is exact — not an approximation — and it naturally leads to an integral representation over option strikes.

Let $H(S)$ be a twice continuously differentiable function, and let $S^*$ be any reference point (later, the current stock price). Taylor’s theorem with first-order approximation and integral remainder states:

$$H(S) = H(S^) + H’(S^)(S - S^) + \int_{S^}^{\infty} H’’(K)(S - K)^+ , dK + \int_0^{S^*} H’’(K)(K - S)^+ , dK$$

Let’s derive this from scratch.

Derivation. Start with the fundamental theorem of calculus applied twice. For any $S > S^*$:

$$H(S) - H(S^) = \int_{S^}^{S} H’(u) , du$$

Now integrate the right side by parts, writing $H’(u) = \frac{d}{du}[H(u)]$ and inserting a dummy integration-by-parts identity. Alternatively, use the standard second-order Taylor formula and write the remainder as an integral using the Cauchy form. The cleanest derivation is as follows.

Write the remainder after a first-order Taylor expansion:

$$H(S) = H(S^) + H’(S^)(S - S^*) + R(S)$$

The Lagrange remainder for a first-order expansion is:

$$R(S) = \int_{S^*}^{S} H’’(u)(S - u) , du$$

This is the exact integral form of the second-order remainder. Verify by differentiating under the integral with respect to $S$:

$$\frac{d}{dS} R(S) = H’’(S) \cdot 0 + \int_{S^}^{S} H’’(u) \frac{\partial}{\partial S}(S - u) , du = \int_{S^}^{S} H’’(u) , du = H’(S) - H’(S^*)$$

which is exactly right. And $R(S^*) = 0$. So the formula is exact.

Now extend this to allow $S$ to be either above or below $S^*$. Combine both branches:

$$H(S) = H(S^) + H’(S^)(S - S^) + \int_{S^}^{\infty} H’’(K)(S - K)^+ , dK + \int_0^{S^*} H’’(K)(K - S)^+ , dK \tag{1}$$

Why does this work on both sides? When $S > S^$, only the first integral contributes because $(K - S)^+ = 0$ for $K < S^$ and the second integral vanishes; similarly $(S - K)^+ = S - K$ for $K \in (S^, S)$ and zero for $K > S$. When $S < S^$, by symmetric reasoning only the second integral contributes.

This is equation (1): a decomposition of any smooth payoff into a bond position, a stock position, and a continuous strip of calls and puts weighted by the second derivative of the payoff. The weight on a call at strike $K > S^$ is $H’’(K)$, and the weight on a put at strike $K < S^$ is $H’’(K)$.

Pricing a Claim via Static Replication — Equation (2)#

Setting Up the Framework#

Let $S_t$ be the stock price at time $t$, and let $S = S_{t+\tau}$ be the stock price at the horizon $t + \tau$. Denote the risk-neutral density of $S$ by $q(S)$, and write the risk-neutral expectation operator as $\mathbb{E}^*_t[\cdot]$.

The price of a European call at strike $K$ is:

$$C(t, K) = e^{-r\tau} \int_0^\infty (S - K)^+ q(S) , dS$$

and similarly for a put $P(t, K) = e^{-r\tau} \int_0^\infty (K - S)^+ q(S) , dS$.

Applying the Taylor Decomposition to Pricing#

Take equation (1) and apply the risk-neutral pricing operator $e^{-r\tau} \mathbb{E}^_t[\cdot]$ to both sides. The left side becomes the arbitrage-free price $e^{-r\tau} \mathbb{E}^_t[H(S)]$. On the right side:

  • The term $H(S^)$ is a constant, so $e^{-r\tau} \mathbb{E}^_t[H(S^)] = e^{-r\tau} H(S^)$, which is the value of a zero-coupon bond paying $H(S^*)$.
  • The term $H’(S^)(S - S^)$ is linear in $S$. Its expectation gives $H’(S^)(e^{r\tau} S_t - S^)$, since the forward price is $S_t e^{r\tau}$ under risk-neutral measure. So $e^{-r\tau} \mathbb{E}^_t[H’(S^)(S - S^)] = H’(S^)(S_t - S^* e^{-r\tau})$.
  • The integral of calls: $e^{-r\tau} \mathbb{E}^t\left[\int{S^}^\infty H’’(K)(S-K)^+ dK\right] = \int_{S^*}^\infty H’’(K) C(t, K) , dK$.
  • The integral of puts: $e^{-r\tau} \mathbb{E}^_t\left[\int_0^{S^} H’’(K)(K-S)^+ dK\right] = \int_0^{S^} H’’(K) P(t, K) , dK$. Choosing $S^ = S_t$ (the current stock price), the stock position simplifies because $H’(S_t)(S_t - S_t e^{-r\tau}) = H’(S_t) S_t (1 - e^{-r\tau})$, but more directly, recalling the no-arbitrage relation $e^{-r\tau} \mathbb{E}^*_t[S] = S_t$, the stock component evaluates to $H’(S_t) \cdot S_t (1 - e^{-r\tau})$. In BKM notation, combining the bond and stock components, the pricing formula becomes:

$$e^{-r\tau} \mathbb{E}^*t[H(S)] = \left[H(S_t) - S_t H’(S_t)\right] e^{-r\tau} + H’(S_t) S_t + \int{S_t}^\infty H’’(K) C(t, K) , dK + \int_0^{S_t} H’’(K) P(t, K) , dK \tag{2}$$

This is BKM equation (3). In words: the price of any twice-differentiable claim equals (i) a position in a bond, (ii) a position in the stock itself, and (iii) a continuum of calls and puts weighted by the second derivative of the payoff. This is exact — no model assumption has been made beyond the existence of a risk-neutral measure. The result holds for any risk-neutral distribution, any stochastic volatility model, any jump model.

The economic content is transparent: you are buying the curvature of the payoff function. The second derivative $H’’(K)$ tells you how much of the option at strike $K$ you need to hold in order to replicate the payoff.

The Payoff Functions for Vol, Skewness, and Kurtosis Contracts#

BKM define the horizon return as the log price ratio:

$$R_{t,\tau} = \ln\frac{S_{t+\tau}}{S_t}$$

They then propose three contracts whose payoffs are defined by powers of this return:

Contract Payoff $H(S)$
Volatility $H(S) = R_{t,\tau}^2 = \left(\ln \frac{S}{S_t}\right)^2$
Cubic (skewness) $H(S) = R_{t,\tau}^3 = \left(\ln \frac{S}{S_t}\right)^3$
Quartic (kurtosis) $H(S) = R_{t,\tau}^4 = \left(\ln \frac{S}{S_t}\right)^4$

Define their risk-neutral discounted values:

$$V(t, \tau) = e^{-r\tau} \mathbb{E}^t\left[R{t,\tau}^2\right], \quad W(t, \tau) = e^{-r\tau} \mathbb{E}^t\left[R{t,\tau}^3\right], \quad X(t, \tau) = e^{-r\tau} \mathbb{E}^*t\left[R{t,\tau}^4\right]$$

These are the fair values of the three contracts. Given equation (2), each can be replicated by a static portfolio of options — once we compute the first and second derivatives of each payoff and substitute into the formula.

The choice of log returns is deliberate. Using $H(S) = (S/S_t - 1)^k$ would lead to different and messier weights. Log returns give weights that depend only on the log-moneyness $\ln(K/S_t)$, which simplifies both the formulas and the economic interpretation.

Computing the Option Weights#

Step 1: Define the Log-Moneyness Variable#

Let $x = \ln(S/S_t)$, so $H(S) = x^k$ for $k = 2, 3, 4$. The derivatives are:

$$H’(S) = \frac{dH}{dS} = \frac{kx^{k-1}}{S}, \qquad H’’(S) = \frac{d^2H}{dS^2} = \frac{k(k-1)x^{k-2} - kx^{k-1}}{S^2}$$

Using the chain rule: $\frac{d}{dS}\left[\ln(S/S_t)\right]^k = k \left(\ln\frac{S}{S_t}\right)^{k-1} \cdot \frac{1}{S}$.

And the second derivative:

$$H’’(K) = \frac{k(k-1)\left(\ln\frac{K}{S_t}\right)^{k-2} - k\left(\ln\frac{K}{S_t}\right)^{k-1}}{K^2}$$

evaluated at the strike $K$.

Step 2: The Volatility Contract ($k = 2$)#

$$H(S) = \left(\ln\frac{S}{S_t}\right)^2$$

First derivative at $S_t$: $H’(S_t) = 2\ln(S_t/S_t)/S_t = 0$.

Second derivative at strike $K$:

$$H’’(K) = \frac{2\left(\ln\frac{K}{S_t}\right)^0 \cdot 1 - 2\left(\ln\frac{K}{S_t}\right)^1}{K^2} = \frac{2 - 2\ln\frac{K}{S_t}}{K^2} = \frac{2\left(1 - \ln\frac{K}{S_t}\right)}{K^2}$$

For calls ($K > S_t$, write $\ln(K/S_t) > 0$):

$$H’’(K) = \frac{2\left(1 - \ln\frac{K}{S_t}\right)}{K^2}$$

For puts ($K < S_t$, write $\ln(S_t/K) > 0$):

$$H’’(K) = \frac{2\left(1 + \ln\frac{S_t}{K}\right)}{K^2}$$

Since $H’(S_t) = 0$, the bond and stock positions vanish, and the replicating price is:

$$\boxed{V(t, \tau) = \int_{S_t}^\infty \frac{2\left(1 - \ln\frac{K}{S_t}\right)}{K^2} C(t, K) , dK + \int_0^{S_t} \frac{2\left(1 + \ln\frac{S_t}{K}\right)}{K^2} P(t, K) , dK}$$

This is BKM equation (7). The weights on both calls and puts are always positive (since $\ln(K/S_t) \leq 1$ for calls near-the-money, and the term $1 + \ln(S_t/K)$ is always positive). The weight is largest near-the-money and decreasing for deep out-of-the-money options.

Step 3: The Cubic Contract ($k = 3$)#

$$H(S) = \left(\ln\frac{S}{S_t}\right)^3$$

First derivative at $S_t$: $H’(S_t) = 3 \cdot 0^2 / S_t = 0$.

Second derivative at strike $K$:

$$H’’(K) = \frac{3 \cdot 2 \left(\ln\frac{K}{S_t}\right) - 3\left(\ln\frac{K}{S_t}\right)^2}{K^2} = \frac{6\ln\frac{K}{S_t} - 3\left(\ln\frac{K}{S_t}\right)^2}{K^2}$$

For calls ($K > S_t$): the weight $H’’(K)$ can be positive or negative depending on how far $K$ is from $S_t$. For puts, flip the sign of the log-moneyness:

$$H’’(K) = \frac{-6\ln\frac{S_t}{K} - 3\left(\ln\frac{S_t}{K}\right)^2}{K^2}$$

The cubic contract price is:

$$\boxed{W(t, \tau) = \int_{S_t}^\infty \frac{6\ln\frac{K}{S_t} - 3\left(\ln\frac{K}{S_t}\right)^2}{K^2} C(t, K) , dK - \int_0^{S_t} \frac{6\ln\frac{S_t}{K} + 3\left(\ln\frac{S_t}{K}\right)^2}{K^2} P(t, K) , dK}$$

This is BKM equation (8). Note the minus sign before the put integral. The cubic contract is a long call / short put structure. When the risk-neutral distribution is left-skewed, OTM puts are expensive and OTM calls are cheap, so $W < 0$. This sign captures the direction of skewness.

Step 4: The Quartic Contract ($k = 4$)#

$$H(S) = \left(\ln\frac{S}{S_t}\right)^4$$

First derivative at $S_t$: zero.

Second derivative at strike $K$:

$$H’’(K) = \frac{12\left(\ln\frac{K}{S_t}\right)^2 - 4\left(\ln\frac{K}{S_t}\right)^3}{K^2}$$

The quartic contract price is:

$$\boxed{X(t, \tau) = \int_{S_t}^\infty \frac{12\left(\ln\frac{K}{S_t}\right)^2 - 4\left(\ln\frac{K}{S_t}\right)^3}{K^2} C(t, K) , dK + \int_0^{S_t} \frac{12\left(\ln\frac{S_t}{K}\right)^2 + 4\left(\ln\frac{S_t}{K}\right)^3}{K^2} P(t, K) , dK}$$

This is BKM equation (9). The quartic contract is always long both calls and puts (the weights are dominated by the squared term for strikes near-the-money). Heavy tails make both deep OTM calls and deep OTM puts expensive, driving up $X$.

Summary of Option Weights#

For a strike $K > S_t$, define log-moneyness $m = \ln(K/S_t) > 0$. For a strike $K < S_t$, define $m = \ln(S_t/K) > 0$.

Contract Weight on OTM calls ($K > S_t$) Weight on OTM puts ($K < S_t$)
Volatility $\frac{2(1 - m)}{K^2}$ $\frac{2(1 + m)}{K^2}$
Cubic $\frac{6m - 3m^2}{K^2}$ $-\frac{6m + 3m^2}{K^2}$
Quartic $\frac{12m^2 - 4m^3}{K^2}$ $\frac{12m^2 + 4m^3}{K^2}$

A few observations are worth noting. First, all weights scale by $1/K^2$, which means the raw dollar value of the option gets deflated by the square of the strike. Second, far out-of-the-money options (large $m$) receive relatively small weights for the volatility contract, modest weights for the cubic, and increasing weights for the quartic. The quartic is especially sensitive to tail options. Third, the cubic contract assigns opposite signs to calls and puts, whereas the volatility and quartic contracts are long both sides.

From Contract Prices to Risk-Neutral Moments#

The Mean Return#

Before assembling the moments, we need the risk-neutral mean return $\mu_t = \mathbb{E}^*t[R{t,\tau}]$. By no-arbitrage:

$$e^{r\tau} = \mathbb{E}^_t\left[\frac{S}{S_t}\right] = \mathbb{E}^t\left[e^{R{t,\tau}}\right]$$

This does not directly give $\mathbb{E}^*t[R{t,\tau}]$ because $e^{R_{t,\tau}} \neq 1 + R_{t,\tau}$ in general. BKM compute $\mu_t$ using a separate application of the spanning formula with $H(S) = \ln(S/S_t)$, which gives:

$$\mu_t \equiv e^{r\tau} \mathbb{E}^*t[R{t,\tau}] = e^{r\tau} - 1 - \frac{e^{r\tau}}{2}V(t,\tau) - \frac{e^{r\tau}}{6}W(t,\tau) - \frac{e^{r\tau}}{24}X(t,\tau) - \ldots$$

In practice, $\mu_t$ is approximated from the same contract prices. The key point is that $\mu_t$ is itself computable from option prices, requiring no additional model assumptions.

Risk-Neutral Variance, Skewness, and Kurtosis#

The centered moments are defined in the standard way. Write $\hat{R} = R_{t,\tau} - \mathbb{E}^*t[R{t,\tau}]$ for the demeaned return.

Variance:

$$\text{Var}^_t = \mathbb{E}^_t\left[\hat{R}^2\right] = \mathbb{E}^_t\left[R^2\right] - \left(\mathbb{E}^_t[R]\right)^2$$

In terms of contract prices:

$$\text{Var}^*_t = e^{r\tau} V(t,\tau) - \mu_t^2$$

Skewness (the third standardized moment):

$$\text{SKEW}_t = \frac{\mathbb{E}^_t[\hat{R}^3]}{\left(\mathbb{E}^_t[\hat{R}^2]\right)^{3/2}}$$

Expanding $\hat{R}^3 = (R - \mu)^3 = R^3 - 3\mu R^2 + 3\mu^2 R - \mu^3$ and taking expectations:

$$\mathbb{E}^*_t[\hat{R}^3] = e^{r\tau}W - 3\mu_t e^{r\tau} V + 2\mu_t^3$$

Therefore:

$$\boxed{\text{SKEW}_t = \frac{e^{r\tau}W(t,\tau) - 3\mu_t e^{r\tau}V(t,\tau) + 2\mu_t^3}{\left(e^{r\tau}V(t,\tau) - \mu_t^2\right)^{3/2}}}$$

This is BKM equation (5). The numerator measures the third centered moment of the log return distribution. When the distribution is left-skewed, OTM puts are expensive, which makes $W < 0$ and drives the numerator negative.

Kurtosis (the fourth standardized moment):

$$\text{KURT}_t = \frac{\mathbb{E}^_t[\hat{R}^4]}{\left(\mathbb{E}^_t[\hat{R}^2]\right)^{2}}$$

Expanding $(R - \mu)^4$ and taking expectations:

$$\mathbb{E}^*_t[\hat{R}^4] = e^{r\tau}X - 4\mu_t e^{r\tau}W + 6\mu_t^2 e^{r\tau}V - 3\mu_t^4$$

Therefore:

$$\boxed{\text{KURT}_t = \frac{e^{r\tau}X(t,\tau) - 4\mu_t e^{r\tau}W(t,\tau) + 6\mu_t^2 e^{r\tau}V(t,\tau) - 3\mu_t^4}{\left(e^{r\tau}V(t,\tau) - \mu_t^2\right)^{2}}}$$

This is BKM equation (6). The quartic contract $X$ captures the tail behavior of the distribution. Heavy tails inflate both deep OTM calls and puts, so $X$ is large when tails are fat, driving kurtosis up.

Connecting the Chain of Reasoning#

It is worth pausing to appreciate the full chain of logic:

  1. Taylor’s theorem (equation 1) shows that any smooth payoff can be written as a bond, a stock, and a continuum of options.
  2. Risk-neutral pricing (equation 2) translates this into a model-free pricing formula: the value of a complex claim is the integral of option prices weighted by the payoff’s second derivative.
  3. Log-return payoffs (equation 3 in BKM notation) are chosen for the three contracts because they lead to clean, interpretable weights.
  4. Option weights (equations 7, 8, 9) follow from computing $H’’(K)$ for each contract — this is pure calculus.
  5. Moment formulas (equations 5, 6) follow from standard moment algebra, expressing centered moments in terms of the raw contract prices $V$, $W$, $X$ and the risk-neutral mean $\mu_t$. At every step, no distributional assumption has been made. The only inputs are observed option prices across strikes. The risk-neutral moments drop out as weighted sums over the option cross-section.

Practical Implementation#

In practice, the integrals are discretized over available strikes. For the cubic contract, for example:

$$W(t,\tau) \approx \sum_{K_j > S_t} \frac{6\ln\frac{K_j}{S_t} - 3\left(\ln\frac{K_j}{S_t}\right)^2}{K_j^2} C(t, K_j) \Delta K + \text{(put sum)}$$

The discretization introduces approximation error, particularly if strikes are sparse or the option grid does not extend far into the tails. BKM show that, because the quartic weights rise with moneyness, deep out-of-the-money options matter more for the kurtosis estimate. For the volatility estimate, the weights are more concentrated near the money.

One important feature of this implementation is that only out-of-the-money options are needed. Calls with $K > S_t$ and puts with $K < S_t$ are used, which avoids reliance on in-the-money options that may have lower liquidity and wider bid-ask spreads. This makes the method practically implementable on standard option data.

Summary#

The BKM framework rests on a single foundational result: any smooth payoff function admits an exact representation as a portfolio of bonds, stock, and vanilla options, with option weights proportional to the second derivative of the payoff. Applying this to payoffs defined by powers of the log return yields model-free formulas for the volatility, cubic, and quartic contracts. These can be synthesized from observed OTM option prices across strikes. The risk-neutral variance, skewness, and kurtosis of the return distribution then follow from standard moment algebra applied to these contract prices.

The result is a set of quantities — risk-neutral vol, skewness, and kurtosis — that are directly observable from the market, require no model calibration, and adapt in real time as option prices move. Rather than asking “what model fits the smile today?”, the BKM approach asks “what does the market collectively believe about the shape of the return distribution?”, and provides a clean, tractable answer.
